# CariCare Grid — Who Can See a Patient's Record, and Why
### One-page summary for health-ministry review

The Grid lets a patient's health record follow them between clinics, hospitals
and islands. The question that matters is not whether the record can move — it
is who is allowed to look, for how long, and how the patient finds out. Six
rules govern that.

**1. Two different kinds of cross-border permission, because there are two
different situations.**
When a Kingston hospital and Trinidad General run a standing cardiology
referral pipeline, asking the patient to tap "approve" for each transfer
protects no one — they were clinically referred into that pathway, and consent
fatigue teaches people to approve everything. Those recurring relationships run
on a signed institutional agreement between the two facilities: named purpose,
named specialties, a fixed expiry of no more than 24 months, annual review, no
onward transfer, and a patient opt-out. Genuinely discretionary access — a
specialist elsewhere who wants a second look, research, insurance, anything
outside an established pathway — still requires that patient's explicit
consent. *Problem solved: routine care stops being blocked by meaningless taps,
while real discretionary access stays under the patient's control.*

**2. "Treating facility" now has an expiry date.**
Previously, one emergency-room visit gave a hospital permanent access. Now
access opens on a specific recorded event — admission, ED intake, a scheduled
appointment, an accepted referral — and closes on a set schedule: 7 days after
an ED discharge, 30 days after an inpatient stay, 90 days for a specialist
clinic, a rolling year for the patient's own community clinic. Any genuine
return visit reopens it. Facilities can shorten these; lengthening them
requires a logged administrative decision visible to the ministry. *Problem
solved: the record is open for exactly as long as there is care to deliver, and
not for years afterwards.*

**3. The most sensitive parts of the record are walled off separately.**
Mental health notes, HIV status, sexual and reproductive health, substance use
treatment, sexual-violence documentation, genetic risk and adolescent
confidential services are each tagged and gated on their own. Being inside an
active care relationship is not enough to open them — they need the patient's
specific permission or a directly involved specialist. A clinician who cannot
see a section is shown that a restricted section exists rather than a chart
that silently looks complete, and safety-critical items such as allergies and
active medications are always visible without revealing the underlying
diagnosis. *Problem solved: in this region, disclosure of these categories can
cost someone their job, their family or their safety; a cardiologist treating
blood pressure does not need any of it.*

**4. Being employed by the facility does not mean seeing the chart.**
Access is scoped to the care team and to role. The attending clinician sees the
full general chart. A consulting specialist sees the referral question and what
is relevant to their specialty. Nursing staff see the current episode's vitals,
medications, care plan and orders. Pharmacy, lab and imaging see only their own
stream plus allergies. Registration staff see appointments and demographics and
no clinical content at all. Facility administrators see no clinical content by
default and cannot grant it to themselves. *Problem solved: "the hospital has
access" was never an acceptable answer; the care team has access.*

**5. Emergency override exists, is narrow, and is always reviewed.**
A licensed clinician can break glass to reach a record immediately when a life
is at stake. It requires a written justification and captures their licence
number, it lasts 24 hours and cannot quietly become ongoing access, the most
sensitive categories need a second clinician's co-signature, and the patient is
notified within the hour with the clinician's name and stated reason. Every
override goes to a mandatory governance review within 72 hours; unjustified
overrides are reported to the ministry, and repeat offenders lose the
capability. *Problem solved: nobody dies waiting for a permission screen, and
nobody uses the emergency door as a shortcut.*

**6. Every patient can see who looked, whether or not they were asked.**
"Who has looked at my record" is a standalone screen, not a footnote inside the
consent page. It lists every access — treating facility, institutional
agreement, consent, emergency override, and refused attempts — with the date,
the facility, the clinician and their role, what was opened in plain language,
and why it was allowed. It cannot be edited or deleted by anyone, including
administrators. Patients are notified automatically the first time a new
facility opens their file, whenever a sensitive section is opened, and always
for an emergency override, and they can flag any entry they do not recognise as
a formal complaint. *Problem solved: because most lawful access on the Grid
happens without a patient tap, visibility after the fact — not the consent
button — is the patient's real safeguard.*

**In short:** consent is reserved for decisions where a patient genuinely has a
choice; institutional agreements carry the routine, structured cross-border
traffic under enforceable terms; care relationships expire; the most
stigmatising data is separately locked; access follows the care team rather than
the payroll; emergencies are served but always reviewed; and the patient can
always see what actually happened to their record.
